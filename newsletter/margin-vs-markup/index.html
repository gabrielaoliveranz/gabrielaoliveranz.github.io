Pricing

Margin or markup? They're not the same number

Kia ora,

Plenty of owners set prices by feel: "that seems about right." Feel is fine, until two similar-sounding numbers get mixed up and quietly cost you money.

Those two numbers are margin and markup. They sound alike, they use the same dollars, and they are not the same percentage.

The same sale, two different percentages

  • $30Profit on an $80 sale that cost $50
  • 60%Markup (profit as a share of cost)
  • 37.5%Margin (profit as a share of price)

Source: Xero New Zealand, Gross profit margin guide (worked example, published 27 May 2026).

Markup is measured against what you paid. Margin is measured against what you charge. Xero's guide puts it plainly: a 50% markup does not equal a 50% margin.

Check 1Which one are you using to set prices?

If you want to keep half of every sale as profit, you need a 50% margin. Many people add 50% to their cost and assume they have it.

Example (invented numbers)

What the item costs you
$100
Price with a 50% markup
$150
Margin you actually get
33%

To keep 50% of the price, the $100 item would need to sell for $200. That is a 100% markup. Same dollars, very different price.

Check 2Look at each product or service, not just the total

Business.govt.nz says a healthier business knows whether it earns enough to cover expenses, both overall and for each income stream, and tracks all the costs for each sale. It describes contribution margin as the version for one product line or service.

Example (invented numbers): a small cafe, last quarter

Coffee and food
40% margin
Catering orders
5% margin
Whole business
31% margin

The overall number looks healthy and hides the problem. Catering uses a lot of staff time and barely covers its own costs.

A product doesn't need a huge margin to earn its place. Business.govt.nz's test is whether it covers its own costs and helps pay your fixed costs. The cafe's catering only just does the first.

Try this · 15 minutes

  1. Pick your three best-selling products or servicesThe ones that bring in the most money, not the ones you like most.
  2. Write the price and every direct cost for eachMaterials, the labour time that goes into it, freight and packaging. Leave out rent and marketing.
  3. Work out the marginPrice minus costs, divided by price. Compare it with the margin you assumed.

Business.govt.nz names two ways to improve a margin: raise prices where customers will pay more, or cut costs.

Worth knowing

  • Use the same time period for price and costs. Xero lists mismatched periods, such as one month of sales against a quarter of costs, among the common margin mistakes. Xero NZ, Gross profit margin
  • Business.govt.nz has a free modelling workbook. It's an Excel spreadsheet you can put your own numbers into. Key equations for your finances

Figures: Xero NZ (margin and markup example). Guidance: Business.govt.nz, Strategic finance and Key equations for your finances (reviewed June 2025). General information, not financial advice. Check anything specific to your business with your accountant.
