Pricing and margins

Costs went up. Did your prices keep up?

Kia ora,

Most small business owners I talk to know their costs have gone up this year. Fewer know exactly where it has hurt.

That matters, because it means price rises tend to go on everything at once, or not at all.

New Zealand small businesses in 2026

  • $1,200More in overheads every month
  • 52%Feel fuel costs through supplier price rises
  • 1 in 3Plan to change their prices

Sources: MYOB Business Monitor 2026 (1,026 businesses) and MYOB fuel survey, March 2026 (237 businesses); Prospa SME Sentiment Tracker, March 2026 (512 businesses).

No surprise, then, that changing prices is now the most common plan among SMEs. The risk is raising them in the wrong place. A flat increase across the board can lose good customers on products that were still making money, while the real leaks stay open. Two places are worth checking first.

Check 1Products whose costs moved the most

Say you sell a product for $100 that costs you $70. Your supplier puts the cost up 6%.

Example numbers

New cost
$74.20
Price to keep the same $30 profit
$104.20 (+4.2%)
Price to keep the same 30% margin
$106.00 (+6%)

Which one you choose is a business decision. You can only make it if you know each product's cost today, not last year.

Check 2Customers whose orders no longer cover the delivery

This is the one that usually surprises people. A customer orders $150 worth of goods every week, at a 25% margin: $37.50 profit before delivery.

Example numbers

Delivery costs you $18
You keep $19.50
Fuel and time push it to $24
You keep $13.50

The delivery cost went up by a third. Your profit on that customer fell by almost a third. Nothing on the invoice changed, so nothing looks wrong.

Small, frequent orders are where rising transport costs hide. Often the fix is not a higher price for everyone, but a minimum order, a delivery fee below a certain order value, or fewer, larger deliveries.

Why this matters now: a third of SMEs expect their profits to fall this year. The businesses that hold their margins will be the ones that raise prices where the numbers say to, and leave them alone where they don't.

Try this · 15 minutes

  1. Export a year of invoicesFrom Xero, MYOB, QuickBooks or your invoicing system, into a spreadsheet.
  2. Find each customer's average orderPivot table: customer in the rows, invoice total as a sum and as a count. Divide one by the other and sort smallest first.
  3. Compare it with the deliveryNext to the bottom ten, write what one delivery or visit costs you. More than about 15% of the order? Take a closer look.

The 15% is a starting point, not a rule. Use whatever line makes sense for your margins.

Worth knowing

  • 15 January is a double date. The second provisional tax instalment is due for most businesses with a 31 March balance date (standard or estimation option), and so is the GST return for the period ending 30 November. Worth setting the money aside before the break. Inland Revenue
  • Two holidays fall on a Saturday this summer. Boxing Day (26 December) and the day after New Year's Day (2 January). For staff who don't normally work Saturdays, they move to Monday 28 December and Monday 4 January. Employment New Zealand
  • Getting paid is still slow. In the June quarter, small businesses waited an average of 24.1 days to be paid, and invoices were paid 4.7 days late on average. Xero Small Business Insights

General information, not tax advice. Check anything specific to your business with your accountant.
