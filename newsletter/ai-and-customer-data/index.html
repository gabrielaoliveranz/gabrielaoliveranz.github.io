Data and privacy

You use AI at work. Where does your customers' data go?

Kia ora,

Most small businesses I talk to now use AI for something: writing emails, summarising documents, tidying up a spreadsheet. Very few have stopped to ask what happens to the information they paste in.

That matters, because a lot of what gets pasted in is about other people: customers, patients, staff.

Who uses AI in New Zealand, by size, 2026

  • 91%Organisations with 100 or more staff
  • 64%Businesses with 20 or more staff
  • 30%Small businesses with 1 to 5 staff

Sources: Datacom State of AI Index 2026 (207 leaders of organisations with 100+ staff); MYOB Business Monitor 2026 (1,026 businesses, share proactively using AI).

Large organisations adopt AI with IT teams, contracts and policies. Small businesses mostly adopt it one person and one free account at a time. Among SMEs, only 13% have an AI policy at all.

Customers have noticed. In this year's privacy survey, 67% of New Zealanders said they were concerned about businesses using AI to make decisions with their personal information, and 82% want more say in how their information is used.

The Privacy Act applies to AI tools the same way it applies to everything else you do with personal information. Two places are worth checking first.

Check 1What is going into the tools today

Say a customer emails a complaint about a late order. To reply faster, someone on your team pastes the whole email into a chatbot.

Example

What was pasted
Name, phone, address, order number and the complaint
What the reply needed
The complaint

The reply would have been just as good with "the customer" instead of a name. Everything extra has now left your business, sits on someone else's servers, often overseas, and in most cases can't be taken back.

Most of the time, the fix is not to stop using AI. It is to take the personal details out before you paste: names become [Customer], numbers and addresses go. It takes ten seconds.

Check 2"We don't train on your data" is only half the answer

Many AI providers now promise not to train their models on what you type, at least on paid plans. That is worth having. It is not the same as your data staying private.

Even without training, your text is usually

Stored
For a period, sometimes 30 days or more, often outside New Zealand
Readable
By the provider's staff in some cases, for example to check for misuse
Exposed
If the provider ever has a breach

There is a legal difference too. Under the Privacy Act, an overseas provider that only stores or processes information for you isn't treated as disclosing it overseas. A provider that uses it for its own purposes, such as training its models, can be, and then it's on you to make sure that information is protected to New Zealand standards (Information Privacy Principle 12).

Free versions are where this goes wrong most often: they are the most likely to use what you type, and the least likely to let you control it. The Privacy Commissioner's advice is simple: if you're in doubt, don't put personal information into AI tools.

Why this matters now: customers are paying attention, and since 1 May 2026 the Privacy Act asks more of businesses that collect information about people from someone other than the person themselves. The businesses that keep their customers' trust will be the ones that can say, in one sentence, where their data goes.

Try this · 15 minutes

  1. Ask your team which AI tools they useNo blame, just a list: the tool, free or paid, and what they use it for. You'll probably find more than you expected.
  2. Check each tool's data settingsLook for training (switch it off), chat history and how long data is kept. If someone uses a free version for work, move that work to a business plan or stop it.
  3. Write one rule and share itFor example: "No names, contact details, health or financial details of customers or staff go into AI tools. Replace them with placeholders first."

One clear rule your team actually follows beats a long policy nobody reads.

Worth knowing

  • A new privacy principle has applied since 1 May 2026. IPP 3A: if you collect information about someone from another source (a referral, a list, a partner business), you need to take reasonable steps to let them know. Ministry of Justice
  • Serious privacy breaches must be reported. If a breach has caused, or is likely to cause, serious harm, you must tell the Privacy Commissioner and the people affected as soon as practicable. That includes information leaked through a tool you use. Office of the Privacy Commissioner
  • The Commissioner has a one-page guide to AI and privacy. It's a good handout for your team. Artificial Intelligence and the Information Privacy Principles

Figures: Datacom, MYOB, EMA (AI policy, 300+ member businesses, July 2026), Office of the Privacy Commissioner (1,245 adults, March 2026). General information, not legal advice. Check anything specific to your business with a lawyer or your industry body.
